Religion for the Ruler - Thoughts on Train to Pakistan by Khushwant Singh

July 2026


The political nature of religion enters Mano Majra from two outside forces in Train to Pakistan. One is the Sikh soldier who corrals the Sikh citizens of Mano Majra to take up arms against their Muslim neighbors. The good Sikh will kill as many Muslims as he can, proclaims the young Sikh soldier from the city. His arrival offers a rupture in a place where Sikh and Muslim ways of living are braided together; the soldier strips religion of its moral force and turns difference into a weapon.

The second, more subtle force of politicized religion in Train to Pakistan is Iqbal Singh: a confused political activist. He sees a time of change and seeks to be remembered for his key role in that great change. However Singh’s actions do not match his desires: at the moment when he can stop the plot of the Sikh soldiers to muder Muslim passengers, he refrains, determining the action unworthy of his death. The odds of being remembered as a martyr for his death in this hamlet are slim. For him, religion is a political tool to gain the fame he desires. No fame resides in Mano Majra.

Author Singh contrasts character Iqbal Singh’s apparent pride and cowardice with the love of Jugga. Jugga is the town hooligan, the son of a killed dacoit. However a relationship he has formed with a Muslim girl, Nooran, reveals his loyalty. When Jugga realizes Nooran is aboard the train to Pakistan that the Sikh soldiers and his own neighbors plan to attack, he sacrifices himself, cutting the knotted rope the Sikhs will use to kill all passengers atop the train. After the rope splits in two, Jugga falls under the train, which rolls on to Pakistan unharmed. The animalistic Jugga (even the phonetics of the chosen name suggest a certain sub-human quality) offers the ultimate act of sacrifice: an ego-less death to defy those who weaponize his faith to destroy those he loves. As it is written, “Greater love has no one than this: to lay down one’s life for one’s friends.” Jugga, the political outcast, offers more moral authority than the pious Sikhs he defies. If faith is love, his faith is true.
